Family law · Hindu Marriage Act, 1955

Divorce under Hindu law: grounds, process and time limits

Jalaj Sharma, Advocate · November 2026 · 5 min read

In short

  1. Divorce is either by mutual consent or contested on a ground in Section 13.
  2. A petition cannot ordinarily be filed within one year of the marriage.
  3. Maintenance, alimony and custody can be claimed in the same proceedings.
  4. An appeal against the decree must be filed within 90 days.

1. What is the legal problem?

You wish to end a marriage solemnised under Hindu law. This applies to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs. Either both spouses agree to a divorce, or one of them does not.

2. What does the law say?

The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 provides two routes:

  • Mutual consent (Section 13B). Both spouses have lived separately for at least one year and agree to divorce. See the guide on divorce by mutual consent.
  • Contested divorce (Section 13). One spouse proves a ground recognised by law.

Grounds available to either spouse include:

  • voluntary sexual intercourse with another person after marriage (adultery);
  • cruelty, physical or mental;
  • desertion for a continuous period of at least two years before the petition;
  • conversion to another religion;
  • incurable unsoundness of mind, or a mental disorder of such a kind that the petitioner cannot reasonably be expected to live with the spouse;
  • venereal disease in a communicable form;
  • renunciation of the world by entering a religious order;
  • the spouse not being heard of as alive for seven years;
  • no resumption of cohabitation for one year or more after a decree of judicial separation, or no restitution of conjugal rights for one year after a decree for restitution.

Additional grounds available to a wife include the husband being guilty of rape, sodomy or bestiality; no cohabitation for one year after an order of maintenance in her favour; and her marriage before the age of fifteen, repudiated before eighteen.

Other rules:

  • No petition can be presented within one year of the marriage, unless the court permits it for exceptional hardship or depravity (Section 14).
  • The petition is filed before the Family Court where the marriage was solemnised, where the respondent lives, where the couple last lived together, or, if the wife is the petitioner, where she lives (Section 19).
  • The court must first attempt reconciliation, usually through mediation.
  • Judicial separation (Section 10) is an alternative to divorce on the same grounds.
  • During the case, a spouse without sufficient income can claim interim maintenance and litigation expenses (Section 24). At the decree, the court can award permanent alimony (Section 25) and pass orders on custody of children (Section 26). In maintenance proceedings, both parties must file an affidavit of their assets and liabilities.
  • Irretrievable breakdown of marriage is not a ground before the Family Court. Only the Supreme Court can dissolve a marriage on that basis, under its special constitutional power.

3. What should you do immediately?

  • Note the date of separation, if you are living apart.
  • Keep a dated record of incidents you may rely on, and preserve messages, emails and recordings.
  • Collect your financial documents; they will be needed for maintenance.
  • Do not post about your spouse or the dispute on social media.
  • Do not remove children from the jurisdiction or the country without the other parent’s consent or a court order.
  • If both of you agree, consider mutual consent; it is quicker and less expensive.

4. What is the limitation period?

There is no single limitation period for divorce, but several time rules apply:

  • One year from the date of marriage before a petition can ordinarily be filed.
  • Two years of continuous desertion before a petition on that ground.
  • One year after a decree of judicial separation or restitution of conjugal rights.
  • 90 days from the decree to file an appeal (Section 28).
  • A divorced spouse may remarry only after the time for appeal has expired without an appeal, or the appeal has been dismissed (Section 15).

5. Which documents should you preserve?

  • Marriage certificate, wedding photographs and invitation card
  • Address proofs showing where each spouse lives (for jurisdiction)
  • Messages, emails, recordings and a dated record of incidents
  • Medical records and police complaints, if any
  • Income tax returns, salary slips and bank statements for the last three years
  • Children’s birth certificates and school records

6. What happens if you miss the deadline?

If an appeal is not filed within 90 days, the decree becomes final and either spouse may remarry; a late appeal is admitted only if the delay is satisfactorily explained. If you continue living together after an act of cruelty or adultery, the court may treat it as condoned (forgiven), and the ground may no longer be available.

Common questions

Can a divorce petition be filed where the wife lives?

Yes. If the wife is the petitioner, she can file it where she is residing on the date of filing.

Is irretrievable breakdown a ground for divorce?

Not before the Family Court. Only the Supreme Court can dissolve a marriage on that basis.

Can the husband claim maintenance?

Yes. Under the Hindu Marriage Act, either spouse without sufficient independent income can claim maintenance.

This guide is general information on the law as it stands in November 2026. It is not legal advice; time limits and outcomes depend on the facts of each case.
