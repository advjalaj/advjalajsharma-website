Police not registering your FIR? What to do

Jalaj Sharma, Advocate · November 2026 · 4 min read

In short

  1. If the facts disclose a cognizable offence, registering the FIR is mandatory.
  2. An FIR can be lodged at any police station, whatever the place of the offence.
  3. If refused, send the complaint in writing to the Superintendent of Police.
  4. If the SP does not act, apply to the Magistrate, supported by an affidavit.

1. What is the legal problem?

You have gone to the police station to report an offence, and the police have refused to write an FIR, sent you away, told you to settle the matter, or taken your written complaint without registering it.

2. What does the law say?

Under Section 173 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (earlier Section 154 CrPC):

  • Where the information discloses a cognizable offence, the police must register an FIR. They cannot first test whether your allegations are true.
  • Information can be given orally, in writing or electronically. An e-FIR must be signed by you within three days.
  • It can be given at any police station, even if the offence happened elsewhere. This is called a zero FIR; it is then transferred to the police station that has jurisdiction.
  • You are entitled to a free copy of the FIR.
  • For offences punishable with three to seven years’ imprisonment, the police may hold a preliminary enquiry, with the permission of an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent, to be completed within 14 days.
  • A preliminary enquiry is also permitted in limited categories such as family and matrimonial disputes, commercial offences, medical negligence, corruption, and cases reported after an unexplained delay. It is limited to finding out whether a cognizable offence is disclosed. If the complaint is closed, the informant must be given a copy of the closing entry, with brief reasons, within one week.
  • Failure by a police officer to record information about certain offences against women, including sexual offences and acid attacks, is itself an offence under Section 199 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita.

The remedies, in order:

  1. Superintendent of Police (Section 173(4) BNSS). Send the information in writing. The SP may investigate personally or direct a subordinate officer to investigate.
  2. Magistrate (Section 175(3) BNSS). If the SP does not act, apply to the Magistrate for a direction to register and investigate. The application must be supported by an affidavit and must show that the SP was approached first. The Magistrate may make an enquiry and will hear the police officer before passing an order.
  3. Complaint case (Section 223 BNSS). Alternatively, a complaint can be filed directly before the Magistrate, who records your statement and that of your witnesses.

A writ petition before the High Court is ordinarily not the first remedy. Courts expect the SP and Magistrate route to be used first, except in urgent or exceptional situations. Where the accused is a public servant acting in the course of official duty, the Magistrate first calls for a report from the officer’s superior.

3. What should you do immediately?

  • Give your complaint in writing, signed and dated, and ask for a receipt or a stamped copy.
  • Note the date and time of your visit and the name of the officer you met.
  • If refused, send the same complaint to the SHO and the Superintendent of Police by Speed Post and email the same day.
  • If anyone is injured, get a medical examination done at a government hospital.
  • Preserve evidence: photographs, messages, CCTV footage, and the names of witnesses. CCTV is often overwritten within days.
  • In an emergency, call 112.

4. What is the limitation period?

There is no time limit for reporting an offence to the police, but unexplained delay is viewed with suspicion and may itself lead to a preliminary enquiry.

For a court to take cognizance, Section 514 BNSS sets limits for minor offences: six months if punishable with fine only, one year if punishable with up to one year, and three years if punishable with up to three years. There is no limit for offences punishable with more than three years.

5. Which documents should you preserve?

  • Your written complaint and the receipt or stamped copy
  • Speed Post receipts, tracking records and emails sent to the SHO and SP
  • The medical examination report, if any
  • Photographs, messages, recordings and CCTV footage
  • Names and phone numbers of witnesses
  • Any reply or closing report given by the police

6. What happens if you miss the deadline?

For offences with a limitation period, the court may be barred from taking cognizance, though delay can be condoned if properly explained. In every case, delay weakens the complaint, and evidence such as CCTV footage, call records and injuries may be lost.

Common questions

Can the police refuse because the offence happened in another area?

No. The information must be recorded as a zero FIR and transferred to the police station that has jurisdiction.

Can I go straight to the High Court?

Ordinarily not. The Superintendent of Police and the Magistrate should be approached first, unless the situation is urgent or exceptional.

Is a Non-Cognizable Report (NCR) the same as an FIR?

No. An NCR is recorded for non-cognizable offences, and the police cannot investigate it without the Magistrate’s permission.

This guide is general information on the law as it stands in November 2026. It is not legal advice; time limits and outcomes depend on the facts of each case.
