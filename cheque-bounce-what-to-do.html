Cheque bounced? What to do, step by step, under Section 138 of the NI Act

Jalaj Sharma, Advocate · November 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  1. Keep the original cheque and the bank’s return memo.
  2. Send a legal notice to the person who gave the cheque within 30 days of learning it bounced.
  3. They then have 15 days from receiving the notice to pay.
  4. If they don’t pay, file a cheque bounce complaint within the next 30 days.

A bounced cheque is one of the most common money disputes in India. The law treats it seriously: under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, dishonour of a cheque given to pay a debt is a criminal offence, not just a civil dispute. But the remedy works only if you follow a strict sequence of steps and time limits. Miss one deadline and the case can fail, however genuine your claim is.

This guide explains what to do when a cheque is dishonoured, in the order you need to do it.

Step 1: Understand why the cheque bounced

When a cheque is dishonoured, your bank returns it with a cheque return memo that states the reason. The most common reasons are:

  • “Funds insufficient” or “Exceeds arrangement”: the drawer did not have enough money in the account. These are the classic Section 138 cases.
  • “Payment stopped by drawer”, “Account closed” or “Signature mismatch”: the Supreme Court has held that these can also lead to a Section 138 case, depending on the facts.
  • Technical reasons such as an overwritten date, a damaged cheque or a stale cheque. These usually call for re-presenting a corrected cheque rather than a case.

A cheque must be presented to the bank within its validity period, which is three months from the date written on it. A cheque presented after that cannot be the basis of a Section 138 case.

Step 2: Keep your documents safe

Before you do anything else, collect and keep:

  • the original bounced cheque (never hand it back to the drawer);
  • the return memo from your bank;
  • proof of the debt: a loan agreement, invoices, bank transfer records, receipts, or messages and emails acknowledging the amount owed.

The law presumes that a cheque was issued to pay a debt, but the other side can try to disprove it. Proof of the underlying transaction makes your case far stronger.

Step 3: Send a cheque bounce legal notice within 30 days

This is the step where most mistakes happen. You must send a written demand notice to the drawer within 30 days of receiving the bank’s information that the cheque was returned unpaid. The notice should:

  • give the cheque number, date, amount and bank;
  • state the date and reason of dishonour;
  • demand payment of the cheque amount within 15 days of receiving the notice.

Send it by Speed Post or registered post to the drawer’s correct address and keep the receipt and tracking record. Sending a copy by email or WhatsApp as well is sensible. If the notice is returned as “refused” or “unclaimed”, courts generally treat it as served, provided it was sent to the correct address.

Step 4: Wait 15 days for payment

The drawer has 15 days from receiving the notice to pay. If they pay in full within that time, the matter ends there. If they don’t, the offence is complete on the 16th day, and your right to file a case begins.

Step 5: File the cheque bounce case within one month

The complaint must be filed within one month from the day the 15-day period ends. A delayed complaint can be accepted only if the court is satisfied there was sufficient cause for the delay, so treat this deadline as fixed.

The case is filed as a criminal complaint before the Magistrate’s court. Since the 2015 amendment, the right court is the one with jurisdiction over the branch of your own bank where you presented the cheque or hold your account. You do not need to file where the drawer lives.

The complaint is filed with your affidavit and the documents from Step 2, along with the notice, postal receipts and tracking report.

The full timeline at a glance

StageTime limit
Present the chequeWithin 3 months of the cheque date
Send legal noticeWithin 30 days of learning of the dishonour
Drawer paysWithin 15 days of receiving the notice
File the complaintWithin 1 month after the 15 days end

What happens after the case is filed

The court examines the complaint and, if satisfied, issues summons to the accused. Cheque bounce cases are meant to be tried summarily, which is quicker than an ordinary criminal trial. The court can also order the accused to pay interim compensation of up to 20% of the cheque amount while the case is pending.

If the accused is convicted, the punishment can be imprisonment of up to two years, a fine of up to twice the cheque amount, or both. If the convicted person appeals, the appellate court can require them to deposit at least 20% of the fine or compensation.

Can a cheque bounce case be settled?

Yes. The offence is compoundable, which means the parties can settle at any stage, including during the trial or appeal. Many cheque bounce cases end in a settlement once the accused understands the consequences. Settling earlier usually costs the accused less.

What about a cheque given as “security”?

A common defence is that the cheque was only a security cheque. The Supreme Court has held that a security cheque can also lead to a Section 138 case if the debt had actually become payable when the cheque was presented. Each case turns on its own facts.

Can I also file a civil case for recovery?

Yes. A Section 138 complaint punishes the dishonour; it is separate from a civil suit to recover the money. You can file a summary suit for recovery under Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure alongside the criminal case. Both can run at the same time.

If you have received a cheque bounce notice

If you are the one who issued the cheque, do not ignore the notice. Paying the cheque amount within 15 days of receiving it prevents the offence from arising at all. If you dispute the debt, reply to the notice in writing, stating your reasons, and keep a record of it.

This article is general information on the law as it stands in November 2026 and is not legal advice. Time limits and outcomes depend on the facts of each case.
